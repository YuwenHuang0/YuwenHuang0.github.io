What the figure shows

  • Peak position: 1.26 → 1.66 → 1.93 → Tc = 2/ln(1 + √2) = 2.269
  • Peak height: 0.41 → 0.65 → 0.87 → infinite (a logarithmic divergence)
  • Phase transition: below Tc the infinite lattice stays magnetised; above it, it does not

In today's ML
  • LLM temperature is this T: logits ÷ T before the softmax
  • Overconfident classifier: it says “90% sure” but is right only 70% of the time
  • Temperature scaling: divide all logits by one number T > 1, chosen on held-out data to minimise the log loss
  • The ranking, and so the accuracy, is unchanged; the stated confidence drops to match reality (Guo et al., ICML 2017)

Station 8 · bridge to Week 4

Sampling: build typical configurations one spin at a time

  • Problem: on a big grid the exact sum over all configurations is impossible
  • Idea: replace the sum by an average. Exact: E[g] = Σs p(s) g(s), 2n terms, each needing Z
  • Sampling: draw s1, …, sN with the right frequencies; then E[g] ≈ (1/N) Σt g(st). No Z, no 2n terms
  • One update: pick a spin at random and look only at its neighbours
  • Local field f = J × (sum of neighbours) + h; set the spin to +1 with probability p = 1/(1 + e−2f/T)
  • Example at T = 2: four neighbours +1 give f = 4, p = 0.982; two against two give f = 0, p = 0.5
  • One sweep = one update per spin on average. The estimate is the average over all sweeps so far

Station 9 · build your own, part 1

Store one picture, damage it, watch it repair

  • So far: the couplings were given. Now the reverse: choose couplings so that the network settles into a picture you pick
  • Picture: one configuration x of the 16 spins. Here it is the letter T
  • Field on spin i: fi = Σj Wij sj, the weighted vote of the other 15 spins
  • Stable: every spin has the sign of its field, so no spin wants to flip
  • To store a picture = to choose W so that the picture is stable
  • To repair = flip a spin that disagrees with its field, and repeat until none does

Click a pixel to damage the picture.

  • Couplings used here: Wij = xixj/16, from the T itself. Two pixels of the T that are equal: +1/16. Two that differ: −1/16. Station 10 derives this
  • Where ±0.94 comes from: each of the other 15 spins votes Wijxj = xi/16. Fifteen equal votes: 15 × 1/16 = 15/16 = 0.9375, shown as 0.94, with the sign of xi
  • With k wrong pixels: a wrong neighbour votes the other way, so a correct spin has field (15 − 2k)/16. Three wrong: 9/16 = 0.56 on a correct spin, 11/16 = 0.69 on a wrong one
  • Stored T: every field agrees with its spin. Energy −7.50, the bottom of the valley
  • Damage a few pixels, then repair one spin at a time: each repair lowers the energy
  • Damage 9 or more: it repairs to the negative picture, which is stable too (the mirror image of Station 5)
In today's ML
  • This is a Hopfield network (Hopfield, PNAS 1982): a Boltzmann machine whose valleys you design
  • Content-addressable memory: you retrieve a whole item from a damaged part of it, not from an address
  • The same retrieval idea, with a softmax in place of the sign, is transformer attention (Ramsauer et al., ICLR 2021)
Station 10 · build your own, part 2

Hebb's rule: how the couplings are chosen

  • Wanted: for every spin, the field fi = Σj Wijxj has the sign of xi. Then the picture is stable
  • Try Wij = xixj / n. One vote is Wijxj = xixjxj/n = xi/n, because xjxj = 1. All 15 votes say xi: fi = 15/16 · xi
  • Compute it: multiply the two pixel values and divide by 16. T of Station 9: row 1 pixels are +1 and +1, so W = (+1)(+1)/16 = +1/16. Row 1 column 1 is +1 and row 2 column 1 is −1, so W = (+1)(−1)/16 = −1/16
  • Several pictures: compute this for each picture and add. Wij = (1/n) Σμ xiμxjμ. This is Hebb's rule
  • The price: a picture now also receives the votes meant for the others. With too many pictures these extra votes win, and the pictures stop being stable

The coupling table: one square for every pair of pixels

  • How to read it: pixels are numbered 0 to 15, row by row, as in the picker. The square in row i, column j is the coupling Wij between pixel i and pixel j
  • Click any square to load that pair into the inspector and see how its value was computed

positive: want to agreenegative: want to differzero: no preference

What to try

  • Two example pictures are stored: horizontal and vertical stripes. Pixels 0 and 2: equal in one, different in the other, coupling 0
  • Forget all and store one picture: every coupling is ±1/16
  • Store more pictures: couplings where the pictures disagree fade to grey. That fading is the pictures competing for the same couplings
In today's ML
  • Hebb's idea (1949), often summarised as “cells that fire together wire together”: a learning rule with no gradient and no error signal
  • Each coupling is a pair statistic of the data, exactly the average E[sisj] that a Boltzmann machine is fitted to
  • Gradient training of a Boltzmann machine starts from this rule and subtracts the model's own pair average

Does entropy reveal when a language model is guessing?

  • Question: invented answers disagree across samples. Does entropy flag them?

Generating data from an energy you cannot normalise

Read: Song and Ermon, “Generative modeling by estimating gradients of the data distribution,” NeurIPS 2019. Ho, Jain and Abbeel, “Denoising diffusion probabilistic models,” NeurIPS 2020.

Alignment as a Boltzmann distribution

  • Question: π ∝ πref er/β. How do reward and diversity trade off in β?

  • First experiment: choose rewards; for β from 0.1 to 10 compute reward, entropy, KL; plot reward against KL

Read: Rafailov et al., “Direct preference optimization: your language model is secretly a reward model,” NeurIPS 2023.
