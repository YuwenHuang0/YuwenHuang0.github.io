Weather on a Ring

Forecasting with learned messages: GraphCast as a toy

Global weather is now forecast by networks that pass messages on a graph. Today: a small version on a ring of stations, what it gets right, and where it fails.

The argument in five steps

  1. The job: forecast 20 weather stations
  2. The trouble: a plain network breaks when we renumber the stations
  3. The fix: one shared message rule on every link
  4. The result: any size, any numbering, but it sees only as far as it passes messages
  5. The frontier: long links, GraphCast, and attention

Each step answers the question the last one leaves open.

Main Question

A network learns to forecast from 20 weather stations. Can it forecast 100 stations, and how far ahead can we trust it?

1 · The job: forecast every station 6 hours ahead

Twenty stations sit on one circle of latitude; each reads its temperature.

SoFrom today's 20 readings, forecast all 20 readings 6 hours later.NextHow does the toy weather change in 6 hours?

1 · The toy weather: a warm patch drifts east and spreads

Every 6 hours, each station mixes its temperature with its two neighbours'.

new temperature =0.5⋅= 0.5 \cdot=0.5⋅ west neighbour + 0.3⋅+\ 0.3 \cdot+ 0.3⋅ own + 0.2⋅+\ 0.2 \cdot+ 0.2⋅ east neighbour

SoThe rule is local: a station needs only its two neighbours. The forecaster is never told it; it sees only 100 noisy pairs of readings, 6 hours apart.NextTry the obvious network first. What does it learn?

2 · The trouble: a plain network reads all 20 stations

Each forecast is its own weighted sum of all 20 readings: 420 numbers, fitted to the 100 pairs.

SoOn new days of the same season, its error is 0.05 °C.NextWhat if the same weather is numbered from another station?

2 · The trouble: renumber the stations

Add 7 to every station number: the warm patch now sits over stations 11 to 15.

Q: Does the plain network still forecast well? A: No. Its error jumps from 0.05 °C to 1.37 °C.

SoWorse than keeping today's values (0.25 °C): it failed.NextWhat did it learn instead?

2 · Why: each station number got its own rule

How much does each forecast use the west neighbour's reading?

SoTwenty rules tied to station numbers, none the true one; and only 20 input slots, so 100 stations cannot even enter.NextCan we force one rule on every station?

3 · The fix: one shared message rule on every link

Each station adds one message from each neighbour to its value.

hi←hi+∑j∈N(i)Mθ(hi,hj,eji)h_i \leftarrow h_i + \sum_{j \in N(i)} M_{\theta}(h_i, h_j, e_{ji})hi​←hi​+∑j∈N(i)​Mθ​(hi​,hj​,eji​)

hi: temperature of station i · N(i): its two neighbours · eji: west or east · Mθ: a small network, 81 numbers θ

SoOne round of messages is one 6-hour step.NextTrained on the same 100 pairs, which rule does it learn?

3 · The fix: every station now uses the same rule

Train Mθ on the 100 pairs; read off the weight on the west neighbour again.

SoOne rule, 0.44 everywhere, close to the true 0.5; the noisy readings pull it slightly low.NextCan a rule trained on 20 stations forecast 100?

4 · The result: trained on 20 stations, run on 100

The same 81 numbers run on any ring: each station still has two neighbours.

Q: Is the forecast on 100 stations worse than on 20? A: Hardly: 0.03 °C on both.

SoA rule about neighbours ignores the number of stations.NextAnd when the stations are renumbered?

4 · The result: renumbering cannot fool shared messages

Add 7 to every station number, as before, and compare the two networks.

SoMessage passing gives the same forecasts, renumbered: the difference is exactly 0.NextHow far can news travel in one round?

4 · The limit: messages travel one station per round

Which stations can change station 11's forecast after 1, 2, 3 and 4 steps?

SoAfter T rounds, only stations within T links can matter: the receptive field.NextWhat if the wind is faster than one station per step?

4 · The limit: a fast wind outruns the messages

The wind now carries air two stations per 6 hours. Warm station 11 by 1 °C.

Q: Does station 13's forecast change in 6 hours? A: No; the true weather changes by 0.30 °C.

SoStation 11 is two links away: its news arrives one step late.NextHow can news travel two stations in one round?

5 · The fix for distance: add long links

Add a coarse second ring: each station also links to the stations two away.

SoLong links carry far news in one round: the error drops from 0.09 °C to 0.03 °C.NextWhat does the real system look like?

5 · GraphCast: the same recipe for the whole Earth

GraphCast (Google DeepMind, 2023) forecasts the globe in 6-hour steps of learned messages.

SoTrained on 39 years of past weather, it beat the leading physics-based forecast on 90% of 1380 tests.NextWhich ideas of Lecture 9 did we use?

5 · The frontier: let every station read every station

Replace the message rule by attention over all 20 stations, as in a Transformer: each station scores every other, softmax, weighted sum.

Q: Does the fast wind’s news now arrive in one round? A: Yes: every station is one link from every other. The price: 400 weights per round instead of 40 links.

SoAttention is message passing on the complete graph (Lecture 9, Part 7). GraphCast chose long links; a Transformer chooses all links. Same network, different graph.NextThe argument in three sentences.

Conclusion

Trouble A plain network learned one rule per station number: renumbering broke it, and 100 stations cannot enter.

Fix One shared message rule on every link works on any size and any numbering.

Limit After T rounds a station has heard only the stations within T links, so far news needs long links.

Frontier Link every station to every station and the message rule becomes attention: a Transformer is this network on the complete graph.

Remember: build the symmetry of the problem into the network, then check how far and how long it can see.

Backup · Push 1: forty rounds, and the weather fades out

To see further, stack more rounds. Take the simplest round, each station ← the mean of itself and its two neighbours, and run it 40 times on one day.

Q: After 40 rounds, where is the warm patch? A: Fading: the spread fell from 5.8 °C to 1.3 °C, by the same factor 0.967 every round.

SoAveraging alone erases the weather (over-smoothing). Our rule survives because each round keeps the station’s own reading: h ← h + messages.NextWhat if the ring has a bottleneck?

Backup · Push 2: one bridge, and news cannot get through

Cut the ring into two rings of 10 joined by one bridge: two valleys and one mountain pass. Warm station 5 by 1 °C.

Q: After 6 rounds, how much of that news reaches the other valley? A: 0.0015 °C across the bridge, against 0.011 °C on the full ring: 7 times less.

SoAll the news of one valley must squeeze through one link (over-squashing). The fix: add links across the pass, as we added long links in Part 4.NextCan the forecaster tell one ring from two?

Backup · Push 3: one ring or two? the forecaster cannot tell

Give the trained rule a uniform day, +2 °C everywhere, once on the ring of 20 and once on two separate rings of 10.

Q: Do the two forecasts differ? A: No: identical at every station, difference 0.000.

SoEvery station sees two neighbours at the same temperature, so it gets the same messages: the rule cannot count stations or see the shape of the graph. For local weather this costs nothing; for a global question, give each station a position input.NextWhat if we only have ten days of data?

Backup · Push 4: ten days of data, against a hand-written solver

Now only 10 training pairs, not 100. The alternative is a solver written from the physics, with the wind speed wrong: badly (0.15) or nearly right (0.25); the truth is 0.3.

Q: Which forecasts best: learn from the 10 days, or keep a solver? A: The nearly-right solver, 0.037 °C; learning gives 0.074 °C; the badly wrong solver 0.110 °C.

SoWith little data, keep a known rule that is nearly right and learn only when it is badly wrong; with 100 days, learning wins (0.030 °C). NeuralGCM keeps the physics solver and learns the rest.NextThe four pushes on one page, with their names.
